# The Body in Tune

*A phenomenological essay on vocal resonance, the felt sense, and what a visualization can and cannot show.*

---

## I. The problem with resonance as metaphor

"Resonance" may be the most overloaded word in common use. It carries distinct meanings across at least four domains: acoustic physics (a cavity responding at its natural frequency), psychology ("that resonates with me"), spirituality ("raise your vibration"), and everyday speech ("there was a resonance between us"). These meanings don't cohere, and the slippage between them has been monetized extensively — the wellness-frequency industry trades almost entirely on the gap between acoustic reality and felt association.

This essay uses the word in the physical sense and tries to mark clearly when it crosses into the phenomenological. The physics is precise and testable. The phenomenology is real but unmeasured. The gap between them is not a failure of understanding — it is the honest position.

The artifact this essay accompanies — Resonant Singer — is a tool for attending to that gap. It shows what the physics model does; it does not claim to show what you feel. Those are different objects, and conflating them would undermine the only thing the tool has going for it: honesty.

---

## II. The vocal folds as source

The larynx is a flow-controlled oscillator. When you phonate — sing, hum, speak — the vocal folds are drawn together by the Bernoulli effect as airflow passes between them, and then pushed apart by subglottal pressure. This cycle repeats at the fundamental frequency: 100–200 Hz for a low male voice, 200–400 Hz for a typical female voice, up to 2000 Hz at the top of an operatic soprano's range.

What the folds produce is not a pure tone. It is a **quasi-periodic pulse train** — a buzz rich in overtones at integer multiples of the fundamental (the first harmonic is the fundamental itself; the second is twice the fundamental; and so on, through the eighth and beyond). The relative strengths of these harmonics — the spectral envelope — is determined partly by the folds' tension and closure pattern, partly by the shape of the airway above them.

This buzz is the raw material of voice. It is not the singing. The singing is what the cavities do with the buzz.

The visualization registers this as a "driver": a frequency and amplitude injected from below, into a system of resonators. Everything interesting happens in what the resonators do in response.

## III. The cavities that answer

Imagine the body above the larynx as a series of coupled rooms — a tracheal tube, a chest cavity, a pharynx, a mouth, a nasal passage, a skull. Each has walls with some combination of rigidity and compliance. Each has a geometry. Each, therefore, has **natural frequencies** at which it responds most efficiently when the walls are set vibrating.

Acoustic resonance occurs when a driving frequency matches one of these natural modes. The cavity then acts as an amplifier, selectively reinforcing the overtone in the buzz that falls near its modal frequency. A singer learns, over years of practice, to shape the vocal tract so that the cavity resonances — the **formants** — land on harmonically useful overtones of the fundamental being sung.

The most important named phenomenon in this system is the **singer's formant**: a cluster of high-frequency energy around 2–4 kHz produced by a narrowing of the lower pharynx that creates a Helmholtz-resonator-like region. This cluster allows a trained singer's voice to carry over an orchestra playing at full volume — the orchestra concentrates its energy below 800 Hz; the singer's formant sits above that, in a relatively clear spectral region. The physics of this is well-documented (it appears in acoustic analyses of trained operatic voices going back to Sundberg's work in the 1970s). The felt experience of engaging the singer's formant is sometimes described as the voice "ringing in the mask" — a distinct buzz in the bones of the face.

The skull also resonates directly through bone conduction. When you hum, the vibration travels from the larynx through the sternum, up the cervical spine, and into the cranium. Bone conduction is more efficient at low frequencies (below ~1 kHz for bone tissue) and is why recordings of your own voice sound different from how you hear yourself internally: your live internal perception includes bone-conducted sound that the microphone doesn't capture.

The chest cavity has its own low-frequency modes — roughly in the 100–200 Hz range — felt as a warmth or resonance in the sternum during low sustained phonation. The specific frequencies of these modes are harder to measure than the skull's — they depend on lung volume, rib tension, and posture — which is why the artifact marks them as `phenomenological` rather than `cited`.

One note of honesty about the "heart zone" in the visualization: the heart does not acoustically resonate at vocal frequencies. It pumps. What the heart zone actually registers is the low-frequency vibration felt in the anterior thoracic wall *near* the heart — a tissue-conduction phenomenon. The icon is anatomically evocative; the physics is about the chest wall, not the muscle.

## IV. Coupling: why the zones don't fire in isolation

If the body's resonators were independent, singing at the chest's natural frequency would light up the chest and nothing else. In practice, this is not what happens — and the visualization is designed to show why.

The resonators are connected. The air column is continuous from the larynx through the pharynx to the mouth. Mechanical vibration travels through bone and tissue from the larynx to the sternum, sternum to skull, skull to inner ear. The vagus nerve — cranial nerve X, the longest in the body — runs from the brainstem through the neck and thorax to the abdomen, carrying both efferent signals (to the heart, lungs, and gut) and afferent signals reporting on their state. When one zone accumulates amplitude, neighboring zones are biased.

This is why the badge in the visualization reads `WHOLE-SYSTEM RESONANCE` when five or more zones activate above threshold simultaneously — not because the model "knows" that's what a full-body resonance state feels like, but because the coupled-oscillator physics predicts that certain fundamentals stack harmonics across multiple zone frequencies at once. Whether that state corresponds to what a singer would describe as "everything ringing" is an open question (AIN-RS-003).

The vagus particle layer in the visualization traces this coupling — a stream of particles moving along the anatomical path of the nerve, faster when the system is active, slower when the breath is at inhale-top. It is a structural representation of the pathway, not a measurement of vagal tone.

## V. The felt sense

Here is where the gap opens.

Acoustic resonance is measurable. A microphone in the pharynx, accelerometers on the sternum, laser vibrometry on the skull surface — all of these can measure the actual vibration. Formant frequencies can be extracted from the acoustic output with high precision. The singer's formant cluster can be confirmed in a spectrum analyzer.

The felt experience of resonance — "that note lands in my chest," "my skull is buzzing," "the voice is flying forward" — is not the same object as the measurement. It is a report from the body's interior sense (interoception) and its sense of mechanical position (proprioception). These signals are real; they are also noisy, subjective, variable across individuals, and not easily mapped to acoustic quantities.

There are several possible accounts of what the felt sense tracks:

One: interoceptive amplification. The chest cavity resonating at its natural frequency produces larger-amplitude wall vibrations than at off-resonance frequencies. These are sensed by mechanoreceptors in the thoracic wall and possibly by the visceral afferents of the vagus nerve. The felt "warmth" in the chest when a low note resonates there might be the body reporting this directly.

Two: bone-conducted vibration in the skull and face. The singer's formant buzz felt in the mask is almost certainly bone conduction — the bones of the face vibrate at frequencies the vocal tract has been shaped to reinforce, and this is experienced as a tingling or ringing quality distinct from the chest sensation.

Three: breath-mediated proprioception. Resonant frequencies at the larynx change the aerodynamic loading on the folds, which changes the muscular effort required to sustain phonation. A singer may report a note as "resonant" partly because it requires less effort — the system has found a configuration where the folds, the breath pressure, and the cavity responses are all cooperating.

What we don't know (AIN-RS-003): whether the badge threshold in the visualization — `sysAmp > 0.55`, `activeCount ≥ 5` — corresponds to anything a person would feel. The threshold is arithmetic. It was calibrated so that it fires rarely and in physically interesting configurations (when harmonic stacking activates multiple zones). It was not calibrated against self-reports. That calibration is the open work.

## VI. Being sung through — the external source case

There is a practice — common in contemplative singing traditions, found in certain voice pedagogy approaches — of humming along with a piece of music and attending to where in the body the confluence seems to land. The song is singing you, in some sense; you are a resonator in its field.

The visualization has a second act for this case. Load an audio file. The song becomes an **external source** — in the model, entering from the top of the skull (a visualization choice for legibility, not anatomy — the UI says so). Your hum is the internal source, rising from the larynx. They interfere.

Where the two sources reinforce, the interference field's amplitude builds. Where they cancel — even at a frequency where a single source would produce strong zone activation — the field creates a **spatial node**: a suppression that the zone reports. The badge can read `◊ SPATIAL NODE` when a zone with otherwise strong resonant response finds itself sitting at a field cancellation point.

This is a physically real phenomenon (two coherent waves canceling at certain spatial locations) rendered in a visualization geometry that is explicitly not anatomy. What would it feel like? Nobody has studied this in a controlled way. The felt sense of singing along with a song at a specific interval while attending to where interference might be occurring — this is not a well-characterized experience in the literature.

What the model predicts: at a perfect unison (same frequency internal and external), the interference pattern is symmetric with clear nodal lines. At a perfect fifth (3:2 frequency ratio), the pattern is more complex and shifts over time as the phase relationship evolves. At inharmonic intervals, the beat frequency introduces a temporal modulation — the field pulses at the difference frequency. These are structural predictions from the wave model; whether any of them correspond to distinct phenomenological reports is the research question (AIN-RS-003).

## VII. Tuning

In a piano, tuning means adjusting the tension of a string until its fundamental frequency matches a reference pitch. In a singing bowl, tuning means shaping the thickness and curvature of the metal until the bowl's natural modes land at desired frequencies. In a voice, tuning is more flexible and more ambiguous.

The vocal tract is continuously tunable. Mouth shape, tongue position, vowel, jaw opening, lip rounding — all of these shift the locations of the formant frequencies. A singer learns to tune these resonators to the harmonic structure of whatever fundamental is being produced. When the second formant frequency matches the second harmonic, the voice gains brightness. When a formant matches the singer's formant cluster, the voice gains carrying power.

The visualization is a tuner in the loose sense: it shows which fundamentals excite which zones. Slide the frequency control toward the chest zone's natural frequency and the chest bar rises. Land on one of the geometric-mean anti-resonance frequencies between zone pairs and the bars drop below baseline — active suppression. Find a frequency where harmonic stacking reaches five zones simultaneously and the badge fires.

It cannot measure what a person's actual vocal tract is doing. It responds to the frequency input alone. The physical reality of tuning — the breath pressure, the fold tension, the cavity shaping — is absent from the model. What the visualization does is show the **resonance landscape** at a given fundamental: where the energy would land if the system were perfectly modeled by ten Gaussian responders with these natural frequencies and coupling coefficients. Whether it corresponds to what a specific body at a specific moment would actually do is, again, the open question.

## VIII. Anti-resonance: the underappreciated case

The left rail of the visualization has buttons marked with a ◊. These are not resonance presets. They are **spectral nulls** — frequencies at the geometric means between adjacent zone pairs where coupled oscillators interfere destructively.

At 147 Hz (the geometric mean of the chest's 120 Hz and trachea's 180 Hz modes), the zone bars don't just fail to rise. They fall *below* their baseline — actively suppressed. This is not silence. It is the model registering active cancellation between two coupled resonators whose responses are out of phase at that frequency.

This phenomenon has a counterpart in room acoustics: the frequency comb of standing waves in a rectangular room has maxima (loud locations) and nulls (quiet locations), and the nulls are as structurally significant as the maxima — they tell you something about the boundary conditions. In voice, spectral nulls in the vocal tract transfer function are responsible for characteristic "dead" qualities at certain frequencies in certain vowels.

The visualization treats these nulls as navigable states. You can sit at 355 Hz (the pharynx×mouth null) and feel the bars go still. What does that feel like? Almost nobody asks. The literature on vocal resonance focuses overwhelmingly on where to put the resonances, not on what happens in the gaps. The ◊ buttons are an invitation to investigate what the gaps are.

## IX. Breath as the carrier

The autonomous rhythm that makes the visualization "breathe" — the vagus particles slowing at inhale-top, the aura slightly dimming and brightening at the breath period — is a synthesized cosine with a default period of five seconds. This is roughly the rate of slow, deliberate resonance-directed breathing: not anxious breathing (15–20 cycles per minute), but the kind associated with contemplative practice (6–10 cycles per minute, sometimes slower).

The model built inside the visualization works as follows: internal source amplitude rides the breath envelope. At inhale-top, the internal drive goes quiet. At exhale-mid, it peaks. The zones respond to the modulated signal, and their first-order amplitude envelopes naturally smooth the entry and exit — the visualization "breathes" rather than stepping on and off.

Whether this is enough to produce an embodied feeling — whether the synth breath alone, without a real breath, can carry the phenomenology — is registered as an open question (AIN-RS-015). Two alternatives are available in the settings: mic-derived RMS (the device microphone picks up your breathing sounds and uses their envelope) and tap-to-breathe (spacebar held during inhale, released during exhale). These are more demanding but potentially more grounding. The prediction — pre-registered in the documentation — is that at least 60% of users who try all three modes would find mic or tap more embodied than synth alone. The experiment has not been run.

The three autonomic rhythms that the visualization traces — heartbeat (~1 Hz), breath (~0.2 Hz), vagus coupling (linking them) — together constitute what the physiology literature calls the **parasympathetic axis**: the branch of the autonomic nervous system associated with rest, recovery, and integration. The visualization is not a meditation tool, a biofeedback device, or a clinical intervention. It is a picture of the parasympathetic axis as a coupled-oscillator system, designed to be looked at while one hums.

## X. What the visualization is and isn't

It is a physics model with hand-tuned parameters, displayed on a canvas at sixty frames per second.

The parameters are honest about their status. Every zone frequency carries an `evidence` field: `phenomenological` (hand-tuned to feel right and produce legible visuals), `pending` (the literature range is known but a specific source is not yet cited), or `cited` (a reference URL or DOI). Currently most are `phenomenological` or `pending`. That is the correct state of the art: the frequencies were chosen to match the broad ranges published in vocal acoustics literature, but they have not been verified against body measurements. They are hypotheses about which ranges matter, not measurements of what's true.

It is not a medical device. The badge does not diagnose resonance disorders or propose therapy. Nothing in the visualization is actionable in a clinical sense.

It is not a biofeedback tool in any validated sense. Biofeedback requires a calibrated sensor measuring a physiological parameter and feeding that measurement back to the user in real time. This visualization measures the frequency of a sound input (from the slider, mic, or audio file) and computes a physics model's response to that frequency. The model is not calibrated against your body.

It is not a demonstration of healing. Resonance — in the physical sense — is a property of oscillators. It does not inherently have health effects. The claim that "resonant frequencies heal the body" is not supported by the physics modeled here or by any well-controlled clinical evidence.

What it is: a tool for attending. For bringing a particular kind of curiosity to what happens in the body during a hum. For investigating the landscape of a coupled-oscillator system that, however imperfectly, represents a part of how the voice works. For noticing that the anti-resonance cases are as interesting as the resonance cases.

The fifteen registered Active Ignorance Nodes are not a list of failures. They are the active boundary of what the model knows — the places where "I don't know" is the more honest output than a number. Attending to that boundary may be the most useful thing the visualization offers.
